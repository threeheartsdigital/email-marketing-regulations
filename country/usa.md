# The United States of America
The USA has some of the least stringent email marketing regulations. The USA's applicable legislation is the 2003 CAN-SPAM Act (Controlling the Assault of Non-Solicited Pornography and Marketing).

## Content required
Commercial emails covered by CAN-SPAM must include a valid postal address, and the subject line must accurately reflect the content. Unless the recipient has given prior affirmative consent, the message must also be clearly and conspicuously identified as an advertisement or solicitation.

## Consent
CAN-SPAM, unlike most other email marketing legislation, works on an opt-out basis. There is no requirement for consent to contact subscribers based in the USA. You must make it clear how to opt out and honour those requests promptly, within ten business days.

If you are sending emails without prior consent, check with your email provider first. The majority of email service providers (such as Mailchimp or EmailOctopus) require consent to contact your subscribers.

## Notable exceptions or requirements
State privacy obligations vary on a state-by-state basis. These laws generally protect residents of the state that enacted them, but they apply only to businesses that meet each law's thresholds and are not covered by an exemption. Around 20 states now have comprehensive consumer privacy laws in force (California's CCPA was the first; Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island took effect in January 2026). These don't override CAN-SPAM's email rules, but they do add obligations around the underlying personal data, such as opt-out and consent requirements.

## Penalties
The CAN-SPAM Act is enforced primarily by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), which can seek civil penalties of up to 53,088 USD per violation (with no maximum penalty). This figure is adjusted for inflation and has applied since January 2025 – there was no 2026 adjustment. In certain circumstances the CAN-SPAM Act may also be enforced by other federal regulators (such as the Federal Communications Commission) for the entities they oversee, by state attorneys general, and through civil actions brought by Internet access service providers. There is no private right of action for individual recipients.
