# Germany
Germany has some of the strictest email marketing laws in the world. The German regulations are defined by the Act Against Unfair Competition (UWG, Section 7 of which governs unsolicited commercial email), the GDPR together with the Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG), and the German Digital Services Act (Digitale-Dienste-Gesetz, or DDG). The DDG replaced the Telemedia Act (TMG) in May 2024 – the legal notice requirements previously in Section 5 TMG now sit in Section 5 DDG.

## Content required
Marketing emails must include contact information and clear identification of the sender in the form of a legal notice. This legal notice can be linked to but must contain the following information:
- The name of the sender and, if applicable, company name
- The authorised representatives for legal entities
- The postal address of the sender (a P.O. box is not sufficient)
- Details enabling rapid electronic contact, including the sender's email address (a contact form can suffice in place of a phone number)
- Any applicable commercial, cooperative, association, or partnership register numbers
- If available, the sender's VAT identification number or business identification number

All of this information must be easy to recognise, directly accessible (linked directly from the email), and permanently available to your recipients. The commercial nature of the message and its sender must not be concealed – there is no legal list of banned subject line words, despite what some deliverability guides suggest.

## Consent
Both the GDPR and the Act Against Unfair Competition require that you have the consent of your recipient before sending them marketing emails, unless they are your existing customer (the soft opt-in under Section 7(3) UWG, which requires the address to have been obtained in connection with a sale, covers your own similar products or services only, and needs an opt-out offered at collection and in every message).

Consent should be collected through a double opt-in registration as you must store records of consent.

In November 2025, the European Court of Justice confirmed that the soft opt-in can also apply where the email address was obtained when signing up for a free account, provided the free account primarily serves as a step towards the provider's paid services.

## Notable exceptions or requirements
As Germany follows GDPR, organisations must appoint a data protection officer in certain circumstances. These include public authorities and organisations whose core activities involve large-scale regular and systematic monitoring or large-scale processing of special-category or criminal-offence data. Germany's Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG) adds further triggers for private organisations: appointment is generally required where at least 20 people are continuously engaged in automated personal-data processing, and regardless of headcount where processing requires a data protection impact assessment or personal data is processed commercially for transfer, anonymised transfer, or market or opinion research. The officer advises and monitors compliance, while the organisation remains responsible for meeting its data-protection obligations.

## Penalties
The maximum fine available under the GDPR is up to 20 million EUR, or 4% annual global turnover – whichever is higher. Under the DDG, concealing the commercial nature or sender of a commercial email can be fined up to 300,000 EUR, and a missing or incomplete legal notice up to 50,000 EUR. Breaches of the UWG's spam rules are typically enforced through cease-and-desist injunctions and damages.
